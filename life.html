Life Beyond the Lab

When I'm not deep in data analysis, I'm usually found on a rock face somewhere. Climbing has been a constant in my life since my undergraduate years at UW-Madison, where I discovered it as a way to cope with stress and connect with a community. What started as a recreational pursuit has become a philosophy—a way of approaching problems, assessing risk, and finding balance between control and letting go. The lessons I've learned on the wall—patience, problem-solving under pressure, and humility in the face of something far larger than myself—translate directly into my work as a researcher.

The vertical world has taken me from the granite walls of Yosemite to limestone cliffs across continents. Each route is a puzzle, each move a hypothesis tested in real-time. The same persistence that helps me work through a difficult research problem keeps me returning to a climbing project until I unlock its sequence.

This page celebrates the adventures, the failures, the sends, and the communities that make climbing more than just a sport—it's a way of seeing and moving through the world.

Why Climbing Matters

Climbing isn't just a hobby—it's a lens through which I understand risk, resilience, and the relationship between humans and the natural world. Every route requires reading the rock, understanding its history, and moving through it with respect and intention.

The problem-solving skills I use on a climbing wall translate directly to my research. Breaking down a complex conservation challenge is remarkably similar to breaking down a climbing route: identify the crux, test different approaches, learn from failure, and celebrate the breakthrough.

More than that, climbing keeps me grounded. It reminds me that I'm not just studying ecosystems from a distance—I'm part of them. The cliffs I climb are ancient, shaped by the same forces I study in rivers and reservoirs. The communities I've found through climbing have taught me as much about human behavior and collaboration as any research paper.

Scientists are whole people. Climbing makes me a better researcher because it keeps me connected to the wonder, humility, and persistence that brought me to science in the first place.
